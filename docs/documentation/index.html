What this is for

A live reading of how far Europe's small businesses have got with AI, built from Eurostat's own surveys — and honest about what those surveys cannot say.

The problem it addresses

The gap between small and large firms on AI is well documented at policy level and almost never translated into something a business owner can read in five minutes. The statistics exist, spread across seventeen Eurostat tables and 291 indicators whose codes mean nothing without a codebook. This turns that into a page.

The argument it makes

The obvious headline — small firms lag large ones — is true and changes nobody's mind. The data supports something sharper: the AI gap is not a technology gap. It is a capability gap, it is concentrated, and it is the kind of thing that can be bought. The portal is organised to make that case in three moves.

1 · It is large, and it is a number

Percentages are arguable. In Italy alone, roughly 195,000 small businesses are not using AI — from 209,000 firms of that size, of which around 14,000 are. The portal shows this as a count of companies whenever the business register and the survey cover the same year.

2 · It is not evenly spread

The gap tracks what work a business does and who it can hire far more than headline size alone.

3 · Non-adopters say why, and it is not the technology

Eurostat asks the firms that considered AI and decided against it. Among Italian small firms: a lack of relevant expertise 59%, cost 45%, difficulty with data 47% — and "AI is not useful for us" only 16%. The obstacle is capability, not conviction.

In every view you pick one or more countries and a band, then choose whether to compare that band against another band or against another country. Multi-country selections combine into a single weighted average, the same way Eurostat builds the EU27 figure. Every chart has a table view behind it, and every link is shareable — the URL carries the whole selection.

Why the numbers can be trusted

Most of the engineering effort here went into not producing plausible nonsense. Four examples of what the portal refuses to do:

It never recomputes the EU27 figure

Eurostat's published aggregate is weighted by how many businesses each country has. An average of the member states is a different number. The portal displays the published one, so it always matches Eurostat's own tables.

It never mixes percentages with different denominators

Not every percentage is out of the same group. "Why firms stay out" counts only the firms that actually considered AI. Two figures that both read 40% can mean quite different things, so each chart states the group it counts.

It marks what Eurostat withheld instead of hiding it

Where too few surveyed businesses fall into a cell, Eurostat publishes no number. Those rows stay on the chart labelled "Not published" — a blank means "too small to measure", never "nobody does this".

It shows gaps, not causes

Everything here is an association. A country doing well may differ in a dozen ways the survey never records. The portal is explicit about this rather than implying that closing one gap would close another.

What it cannot tell you

  • Nothing about businesses under 10 employees. The EU survey does not reach them, though they are the majority of firms in Europe. Every "small firm" figure here means 10–49 employees.
  • No sector breakdown by company size. Eurostat never crosses the two, so "small firms in construction" does not exist in this data — only "firms in construction".
  • No causal claims. See above.
  • Nothing outside Europe, and nothing more recent than the latest survey wave.
